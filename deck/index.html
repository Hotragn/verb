The Verification Budget

An operating model for autonomous AI in project delivery and end to end PMO.

Hotragn Pettugani github.com/hotragn/verb x.com/hotragn linkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani

Open by putting the room at ease. Most people here are not engineers and they have sat through a lot of AI slides this year.

Say who you are in one line, then say what this talk is not: it is not a prediction, not a product, and not a maturity model you have to buy. It is one number and what follows from it.

Tell them the whole thing is free and on GitHub, so they can stop taking photographs of slides and listen instead. Point at the QR at the end.

Part 1

Why the constraint moved

Production stopped being scarce. Review did not.

A short breath before the argument starts.

Say what the three parts are so people know where they are: why the constraint moved, what the operating model looks like once you accept it, and what to do on Monday. Then get straight into it.

The constraint

For thirty years, the scarce thing was getting the work done.

Somebody had to write the status report, chase the risk owners, rebuild the forecast after the change request landed. Capacity meant counting the people who could produce those things.

That is over. An agent produces a defensible status pack for forty projects in the time it takes you to open the file.

Where this comes from
Not a prediction. This is what teams running agents in delivery are reporting right now.
github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani3 / 27

Ask for a show of hands: who has an AI tool writing something that used to take a person an afternoon. Most hands go up. Leave the hands up for a beat.

Then the turn: everyone measured the win in hours saved on production. Nobody measured what happened next.

Do not rush this slide. It is the only one where the audience is entirely comfortable, and the rest of the talk depends on them agreeing with it.

The constraint

The number that did not move is review.

Went up, a lot

  • Decisions produced per week
  • Speed of production
  • How polished the output looks

Did not move

  • People qualified to judge the decision
  • Hours they actually have
  • Time one honest check takes

When production runs past review capacity, the extra work does not stop. It gets approved anyway.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani4 / 27

The right-hand column is the point. None of those three improved when the models improved, and two of them got worse.

Worse how: the artifacts now arrive fluent. A confident, well-written, wrong document is harder to check than a rough one, because there is nothing to snag on.

If the room is senior, this is where you will see the first nod of genuine recognition. Wait for it before moving on.

The constraint

Nobody decides to rubber-stamp.

A reviewer with eleven minutes and nineteen items in the queue produces nineteen approvals.

Every one is recorded as a review. The governance record is intact. The governance is not. There is no error message for this, and it looks exactly like a working PMO right up until something goes wrong and nobody can reconstruct who checked what.

Silent drift. The gap between approvals recorded and reviews genuinely done. Silent because the system records it as success.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani5 / 27

Slow right down here. This is the emotional centre of the talk and it needs space.

Say plainly that this is not a discipline problem and you are not blaming reviewers. The reviewer is behaving rationally under a constraint somebody else handed them. If you get this wrong the room gets defensive and you lose them.

The line to land: rubber-stamping is not a behaviour, it is what running out of review capacity looks like from the inside. Then pause and let it sit.

If you want a story here, use one from your own experience rather than a hypothetical. It has to be real to work.

The verification budget

So put a number on it.

              R x H x u
        VB = -----------
                   c
  • R people genuinely qualified to judge it
  • H hours a week each has for reviewing
  • u share of those hours that survives the calendar
  • c hours to genuinely check one decision

VB is how many decisions of one kind you can genuinely review per week. Compare it against how many you are producing.

Four inputs, and you can get three of them from a spreadsheet this afternoon.

Be honest about R: it is not everyone with the approve button, it is the people whose sign-off you would defend afterwards. That distinction usually cuts the number by half and it is the first uncomfortable moment.

The fourth input, c, is the one nobody has. Flag it now and come back to it, because it is the whole talk in one letter.

Do not derive anything. It is a division. If somebody wants the derivation they can read the repo.

Walk the arithmetic out loud once, slowly. Six times eight is forty-eight, times 0.55 is twenty-six hours, divided by an hour and a quarter is twenty-one.

The four decision classes

Sort work by how expensive it is to check.

Not by risk. Risk tells you whether you must check. This tells you what checking costs. You need both, and most governance frameworks only have the first.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani8 / 27

This is the move people push back on, so say the distinction twice.

A worked pair, said out loud: reconciling a forty million pound budget rollup is high risk and takes a machine nine seconds. Deciding whether to renegotiate a minor dependency with a partner team is low risk and needs somebody who remembers what happened in March. Risk and cost of checking are different axes.

Class D is where you will get a question. Answer it briefly here and properly on the maturity slide: a Class D decision has no finite cost of checking, so there is no amount of it you can afford. That is arithmetic, not caution.

The deployment inversion

Deploy where checking is cheap, not where the task is easy.

deploy
Model is good at it and checking is cheap. This is the whole game and it is less glamorous than the other box.
the trap
Model is good at it and checking is expensive. Every impressive demo lives here. So does every overdraft.

Cheap to check, model is not there yet. Harmless. Revisit next year.
do not
Expensive to check and the model is weak. No path from here.

Vertical

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani9 / 27

Point at the top right and name three things that live there. Portfolio prioritisation. Vendor dispute strategy. Strategic dependency renegotiation.

Say the awkward part: the model is genuinely good at all three. That is not the problem. The problem is that your review capacity for them is a couple of dozen a week, so the deployment is capped at a couple of dozen a week no matter how good the model gets.

You do not fix the top right by deploying harder. You fix it by moving work to the left, which means making the decision cheaper to check. That is engineering work, not a renaming exercise.

Part 2

The operating model

Processes, roles, governance, technology and metrics, redesigned around one constraint.

This is the half of the talk people came for, so mark the transition clearly.

Part 1 was an argument. Part 2 is a design. If they only remember one thing from here it should be that the five layers are not a maturity model to buy, they are five questions that each have one right answer once you accept the constraint.

Say the running time. There are five layers and you have about twelve minutes, so nobody should settle in.

The line worth saying: every one of these layers already exists in your organisation. You have processes, roles, governance, technology and metrics today. None of them were designed around review capacity, because until about two years ago review capacity was not the binding constraint.

Layer 1, processes

The deployment order most PMOs use is backwards.

go first
Easy to build, cheap to check. Start here today. Less glamorous than the box to its right, and the only box that scales.
hold
Easy to build, expensive to check. This is the trap, and it is where every impressive demo lives.
go second
Hard to build but cheap to check. Worth the engineering.
never yet
Hard and expensive to check. Keep it human for now.
Horizontal
task difficulty for the model, easier on the left.

Vertical
verification cost, cheaper at the top.

What most teams do

Deploy along the difficulty axis. Easiest first, hardest last. It feels rational and it is how every roadmap gets drawn.

What actually governs you

Deploy along the verification axis. You can only run as much autonomy as you can check, so cheap-to-check work is the only work that scales.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani12 / 27

This is the same idea as the quadrant in Part 1, turned into a sequencing rule rather than a warning. If you are short on time, say that and move faster here.

The sentence that makes it concrete, and it is worth memorising:

Risk analysis is the easier AI problem. It should go live later than status reporting.

That inverts what everybody in the room has on their roadmap, and it inverts it for a reason they can check. Status reporting is checkable against the source systems in minutes. A risk analysis needs somebody who knows the programme.

Expect pushback on the grounds that risk analysis is more valuable. Agree. Value is not the axis. Value tells you what you want; verification cost tells you what you can run.

The agent role contract

Every agent gets four fields. Not nine.

  • Scope. Which decisions it may make, named one by one. Anything unnamed is out, and running into one is a stop.
  • Evidence. What it must show for every decision. If it cannot produce the evidence, it does not make the decision.
  • Escalation. The named conditions where it stops, and the named human it stops to.
  • Revocation. How you turn it off, who can, how fast, and what happens to work in flight.

Four is the number somebody can hold in their head at six on a Friday when the thing is misbehaving and a call has to be made. That is the only moment the contract has to work.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani13 / 27

Say why the number is four rather than a longer, safer-looking list. A contract gets read twice: once at approval when everyone is calm, and once during an incident. Only the second reading matters, and a fifteen-field contract fails it.

Revocation is the one people leave out, and the one to press on. Ask the room: who here could turn off their AI tooling this afternoon without a meeting. It usually goes quiet.

If somebody says four is too few, the answer is that rate limits go in scope, retention goes in evidence, on-call goes in escalation, and the kill switch goes in revocation. Nothing genuine has needed a fifth.

This is the slide people photograph, so leave it up a beat longer than feels comfortable.

The three existing roles do not disappear, they invert. The project manager stops chasing and starts checking. That is a real change to somebody's job and worth saying plainly rather than dressing up.

The two new roles are the point. Say why they get skipped: they are not technical, so no budget line obviously owns them, and they are accountability rather than delivery, so nobody volunteers.

If asked whether these are full-time: at forty projects, the verification lead is a day a week and the agent steward is two. They are not headcount, they are named responsibility. But they have to be named, because a budget nobody owns is a budget nobody defends.

Notice what is absent. Model confidence appears nowhere on this table. Confident and wrong on an irreversible decision is still a disaster, and a confidence score is the agent marking its own work.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani15 / 27

The ladder is easy to follow, so spend your time on the last line instead.

Almost every governance framework people have seen gates on confidence: high confidence gets more autonomy. Say why that is backwards. Confidence is the agent's opinion of itself, and it is exactly as trustworthy as the thing you were trying to check. Blast radius is a property of the decision, and it is knowable before the agent runs.

If somebody asks how this relates to the four classes: classes tell you what checking costs, blast radius tells you what happens if you get it wrong. Class sizes the budget, blast radius sets the authority. You need both, and they are not the same axis.

The practical version: you can build this table in an afternoon with your existing delegated authority matrix. Most organisations already have one for spend. This is the same idea pointed at reversibility.

The evidence plane

Six things every decision has to carry.

  1. What was decided. One sentence, naming the thing.
  2. What it rested on. The actual sources, not a summary.
  3. What was rejected, and why.
  1. How confident, and how it would be wrong. Named, not hedged.
  2. How to undo it, what that costs, and how long it stays cheap.
  3. Who is accountable. A person, not a team.
A decision missing any of the six is not a decision. It is an output. Outputs do not get actioned.
Why this list
Every field exists to make the next review faster. Field two alone is most of the saving, because it is the reviewer not re-gathering what the agent already had.
github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani16 / 27

The point people miss: this is not an audit trail. An audit trail is built for reconstructing the past months later. This is built for making the next review fast this afternoon. Those two goals produce different documents and only one of them changes the budget.

The evidence for field two: ask any reviewer where the time goes on a hard review. It is not the judgement, it is re-finding the inputs. Typically half.

Field three has a subtle mechanism worth explaining if you have time. Without it, the reviewer's question is "is this right", which is unbounded. With it, the question becomes "is one of these rejection reasons wrong", which is a list you can get to the end of.

Nothing writes back to the source systems until it has cleared verification. The write path is the governance boundary.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani17 / 27

Trace the arrows left to right with your hand once, then stop and talk about the line at the bottom, because that is the whole slide.

Most teams put the governance boundary at the model: what may the agent see, what may it be asked. That is the wrong boundary, because reading is not where the risk is. The boundary belongs on the write path. An agent that reads everything and writes nothing until a check clears is a safe agent, whatever else it is doing.

This also gives you a cheap architectural test that anybody can apply. Ask of any proposed integration: can this agent write to a source system without a verification step in between? If yes, the boundary is in the wrong place, and no amount of prompt engineering fixes that.

Note the analysts are marked propose only. That is Class C from Part 1 showing up in the architecture rather than in a policy document.

Agentic verification

Agents can supply review capacity, not just consume it.

  c_eff = c_a + (1 - k) x c

Put a second agent in front of the human. It closes some decisions on its own, with a machine-checkable reason. k is the share it closes. The budget goes up without hiring anybody.

Three rules, or this becomes wishful thinking

  1. The verifier's own output has to be machine-checkable. If a human has to read its reasoning, you moved the cost, you did not remove it.
  2. Measure how often it misses a bad decision. Unmeasured means it counts as zero. Not an estimate. Zero.
  3. Bank the bottom of the confidence range, never the headline.
github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani18 / 27

This is the part that is genuinely new, so give it room. It is also the part that is easiest to sell badly, so lead with the rules rather than the upside.

Rule two is the one to press on. An agent checking an agent is a lovely diagram and a risk transfer unless somebody has measured its miss rate against decisions known to be bad. Until then it is an opinion with a confident tone.

There is a sting worth mentioning if the room is technical: the verifier closes the easy ones first, so the queue that reaches the human gets harder, so c goes up as k goes up. Re-measure after every change. The repo says the framework has no clean correction for this, which is the honest position.

The six operating metrics

How to measure the thing nobody measures.

Set a floor: the time below which a genuine review could not physically have happened.

Setting the floor

  • Watch thirty real reviews. Confirm afterwards that each one was genuine.
  • Take the tenth percentile of those times.
  • Never go below reading speed on the document itself.

Reading the number

  • A tenth-percentile floor reports about 10 percent even when all is well.
  • So the signal is the excess above 10, not the number itself.
  • 34 percent means a third of approvals are decorative.

The rule that keeps it alive: never use this on an individual. The moment you do, review time becomes a thing people manage rather than a thing you measure, and the metric is dead inside one cycle.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani20 / 27

Say the last line twice and mean it. If your organisation cannot resist using this against people, do not collect it at all. A corrupted metric is worse than a missing one, because a missing one does not tell you everything is fine.

The tenth percentile rather than the average, because you are not trying to catch fast reviewers. Fast reviewers are good. You are trying to catch reviews that could not have happened.

There is a lag worth mentioning: the overdraft turns into backlog first, then late nights, then drift. It usually takes about six weeks to show, which is why people conclude the overdraft was harmless. It was not. It was queued.

This is the most useful slide in Part 2 and it is worth slowing down for.

Every one of those four numbers is a metric a real PMO reports today. Not one of them can see the failure. Worse, three of them move in the direction that looks like success while the failure gets worse: approvals rise because approving got faster, cycle time falls for the same reason, and reversals fall because nobody is looking hard enough to find the errors.

The reversal rate is the cruellest one. It falls, and it looks like quality improving. It is falling because detection stopped.

The four eval gates

Four gates, each with a number attached.

Before it runs

  • Do we agree what class this is? Two qualified people classify fifty decisions separately. If they disagree, you do not know what checking costs.
  • Would it have been right on history? Replay it against decisions you already know the outcome of. Zero Class D taken on its own.

While it runs

  • Is every decision checkable at the cost we assumed? Continuous, plus twenty read by a human for substance.
  • Is the containment real? Quarterly, against decisions known to be bad.

A gate without a number attached to it is a meeting.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani22 / 27

That last line usually gets a laugh. Let it, then make the serious point: every one of these has a threshold in the repo, and the runners are there too, so this is not a slide of good intentions.

Gate one fails more often than teams expect, and that failure is the most useful thing it produces. If two qualified people cannot agree what class something is, every number downstream is decoration.

The replay gate has a criterion people find strange and it is worth the thirty seconds: if the agent disagrees with history and turns out to be right, it still fails unless a reviewer could adjudicate that disagreement inside the budgeted time. A right answer nobody can check is not usable.

Part 3

What to do about it

Where you are, what is missing, and the smallest useful thing you can do on Monday.

Short transition. Signal that the design half is over and the ask is coming, so anybody who has been taking notes knows to stop and listen.

You have about six minutes left at this point. Say it, so people know questions are close.

Maturity stages

Five stages, and the test for which one you are on.

   S0            S1            S2            S3            S4
   unmeasured    measured      bounded       contained     self-budgeting
   |             |             |             |             |
   you do not    you know      you say no    agents supply the system
   know          the gap       to work       verified      throttles
                                             capacity      itself
   |             |             |             |             |
   +-------------+-------------+-------------+-------------+----X  no S5

The test is one question. What does it cost you to check one expert decision, and when did you last measure it?

If there is no number and no date, you are at S0. Most organisations that describe themselves as S2 are at S0 with better paperwork.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani24 / 27

Do not walk all five. Give them the one-question test and let them place themselves privately. That lands harder than a description.

The hard step is S1 to S2, and it is not technical. It is being willing to say no to a deployment that is over budget. Everything before that is measurement and everything after is engineering.

Then why there is no S5, which is the part people remember. S5 would be a system that sets its own risk appetite and audits its own drift: no human in the loop at all. That is not more mature, it is the removal of the thing the model exists to manage.

The line: such a system might still be right. It just could not be known to be right by the people running it, and an organisation that cannot know is not governing, it is hoping.

Known limitations

What I do not know.

  • The measurement is biased. People check more carefully when watched, so the budget always looks better than it is. Direction known, size not.
  • Bursts are not modelled. The budget is a rate. Work does not arrive evenly. You can be fine on average and still drop reviews in board week. This is the biggest hole and I am asking for help with it.
  • Timing is a proxy for checking. Somebody who already knows the answer can approve correctly in twenty seconds.
  • The B and C line is genuinely blurry, and nobody has published how often two people disagree.
  • There is no field data. Every figure in this talk is illustrative. The repository says so on the same page it prints them.
github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani25 / 27

Do not apologise your way through this slide. Deliver it flat and fast. It buys more credibility than anything else in the talk, and a framework that hides its weak points gets adopted and then quietly dropped.

The bursts one is a real invitation. If anybody in the room knows queueing theory you want them to find you afterwards, so say that out loud.

On the last point: be blunt that the numbers are illustrative. Somebody will screenshot a figure and repost it, and you want to have said this clearly enough that it is on record.

What to do

Four things you can do on Monday.

  1. Pick one decision type an agent already touches. One. Not a programme.
  2. Time thirty reviews of it. Then ask each reviewer whether they genuinely checked, and throw away the ones who say no. That last step is the whole thing.
  3. Do the division. Four numbers into the calculator. It takes about a minute and it runs in your browser with nothing sent anywhere.
  4. Show somebody the gap. Not a programme, not a policy. One number, one conversation.

If the gap is under 1.0 you have headroom and can deploy more. If it is over, you now know something you did not know this morning, and that is the entire first stage.

github.com/hotragn/verbx.com/hotragnlinkedin.com/in/hotragn-pettugani26 / 27

This is what they leave with, so make it feel small. Everything before this was the argument; this is the ask, and the ask has to be doable by one person without permission.

Step two is the one that gets skipped and it is the one that makes the number mean anything. Without that question you are measuring how long approvals take, not how long checking takes, and under overdraft those are different quantities.

End on the last line rather than on an exhortation. Knowing the gap is genuinely the whole of stage one, and saying so lowers the barrier.
