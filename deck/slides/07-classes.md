## Sort work by how expensive it is to check.

| | class | the test | cost to check | who decides |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| <span class="chip A">A</span> | machine-checkable | a test decides it | minutes | agent |
| <span class="chip B">B</span> | sample-checkable | check twenty, conclude about two hundred | minutes, spread | agent |
| <span class="chip C">C</span> | expert-checkable | a qualified person rebuilds the reasoning | hours | agent proposes, human decides |
| <span class="chip D">D</span> | not checkable in advance | you only find out afterwards | no answer | human, always |

<p class="small">Not by risk. Risk tells you <em>whether</em> you must check.
This tells you <em>what checking costs</em>. You need both, and most governance
frameworks only have the first.</p>

@notes

This is the move people push back on, so say the distinction twice.

A worked pair, said out loud: reconciling a forty million pound budget rollup is
high risk and takes a machine nine seconds. Deciding whether to renegotiate a
minor dependency with a partner team is low risk and needs somebody who
remembers what happened in March. Risk and cost of checking are different axes.

Class D is where you will get a question. Answer it briefly here and properly on
the maturity slide: a Class D decision has no finite cost of checking, so there
is no amount of it you can afford. That is arithmetic, not caution.
