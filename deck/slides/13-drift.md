## How to measure the thing nobody measures.

<p class="lead">Set a floor: the time below which a genuine review could not
physically have happened.</p>

<div class="cols">
<div>
<h4>Setting the floor</h4>
<ul>
<li>Watch thirty real reviews. Confirm afterwards that each one was genuine.</li>
<li>Take the tenth percentile of those times.</li>
<li>Never go below reading speed on the document itself.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div>
<h4>Reading the number</h4>
<ul>
<li>A tenth-percentile floor reports about 10 percent even when all is well.</li>
<li>So the signal is the excess above 10, not the number itself.</li>
<li>34 percent means a third of approvals are decorative.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>

<p class="small"><strong>The rule that keeps it alive:</strong> never use this on
an individual. The moment you do, review time becomes a thing people manage
rather than a thing you measure, and the metric is dead inside one cycle.</p>

@notes

Say the last line twice and mean it. If your organisation cannot resist using
this against people, do not collect it at all. A corrupted metric is worse than a
missing one, because a missing one does not tell you everything is fine.

The tenth percentile rather than the average, because you are not trying to catch
fast reviewers. Fast reviewers are good. You are trying to catch reviews that
could not have happened.

There is a lag worth mentioning: the overdraft turns into backlog first, then
late nights, then drift. It usually takes about six weeks to show, which is why
people conclude the overdraft was harmless. It was not. It was queued.
